## Prompt (Task 2 - Education & Economics)
> *Some people think that universities should provide graduates with the knowledge and skills needed in the workplace. Others think that the true function of a university should be to give access to knowledge for its own sake, regardless of whether the course is useful to an employer.*
> *Discuss both views and give your opinion.*

---

## Band 9 Benchmark Essay

Whether higher education institutions should function primarily as vocational training grounds or as bastions of pure intellectual inquiry remains a central debate in modern educational policy. While proponents of utilitarian education argue that curricula must align directly with commercial labor demands, advocates of traditional academia assert that the unconstrained pursuit of knowledge possesses an intrinsic value that transcends economic utility. This essay argues that while workforce preparation is undeniably a crucial responsibility of modern universities, subordinating tertiary education solely to market forces risks eroding the very critical and philosophical foundations upon which genuine innovation depends.

The argument for vocational alignment is grounded in pragmatic economic reality. In an increasingly competitive and specialized global marketplace, students and governments invest substantial financial capital into tertiary qualifications with the reasonable expectation of a tangible return on investment. If degree programs fail to equip graduates with relevant technical proficiencies, industry-standard competencies, and professional acumen, both individual employment prospects and national economic vitality suffer. From this perspective, a curriculum detached from corporate demands is an expensive indulgence that leaves graduates ill-prepared for the rigors of modern enterprise.

Conversely, championing knowledge for its own sake preserves the fundamental mission of the university as an incubator of critical thought. Academic disciplines such as philosophy, pure mathematics, and classical literature may yield few immediate commercial applications, yet they cultivate cognitive flexibility, rigorous analytical capabilities, and ethical discernment. It is precisely these abstract, foundational disciplines that have historically produced paradigm shifts in science and human rights. Were universities to discard courses that do not directly generate commercial revenue, society would produce technically adept technicians at the expense of reflective, visionary thinkers.

In conclusion, universities need not view vocational training and pure academic scholarship as mutually exclusive ideals. While tertiary institutions must ensure that graduates possess viable skills to contribute meaningfully to the contemporary economy, they must fiercely safeguard their role as guardians of universal knowledge and philosophical rigor. True innovation arises not merely from fulfilling current workplace requirements, but from cultivating minds capable of reimagining them.
